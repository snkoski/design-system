# How Bounce works

Bounce is three files, each with one job:

Every code block on this page is read from those files when the page loads, so it's always the code that runs. The demos call the real functions too.

## 1. Where it is and how fast it's going

Everything about a moving Bounce is four numbers. `x` and `y` are its position in pixels from the top left corner of its box, and `vx` and `vy` are its velocity: how many pixels it moves per second, across and down. On screens, `y` grows downwards, so a positive `vy` means moving down.

<Code file="experiments/Bounce/physics.ts" name="Body" />

You don't give Bounce a velocity directly. You give it a `speed` and an `angle`, and `velocity()` splits the speed into its across and down parts with trigonometry: cosine gives the share across, sine the share down. At 45°, both shares are about 0.71, so the default speed of 240 px/s becomes about 170 px/s right and 170 px/s down.

## 2. One frame: `step()`

The whole physics engine is one function. Each frame, the loop calls it with the time since the last frame, in seconds: about 0.0167 on a 60 Hz screen, or 0.0083 on a 120 Hz one. Everything is measured per second and multiplied by `seconds`, so it moves at the same speed whatever the screen's refresh rate.

<Code file="experiments/Bounce/physics.ts" name="step" />

It does five things, in this order:

1. **Friction slows it down.** `slow = Math.exp(-friction * seconds)` is a number just under 1, and both velocities are multiplied by it. Section 3 explains why it's `Math.exp`.
2. **Gravity speeds it up downwards.** `vy += gravity * seconds`. Gravity is an acceleration, in pixels per second per second: after one second of falling, `vy` has grown by `gravity`.
3. **It moves.** `x += vx * seconds` and `y += vy * seconds`. The velocity changes before the position does (this is called semi-implicit Euler), which keeps a ball bouncing at a steady height. Doing it the other way round slowly adds energy, and the bounces creep higher.
4. **It bounces off any edge it went past.** If it's beyond an edge and still moving towards it, it's put back on the edge and its velocity is flipped and scaled: `vx = -vx * keep`, where `keep` is `bounciness` held between 0 and 1. The "still moving towards it" check (`vx < 0` at the left edge, for example) matters: without it, something that's just bounced but hasn't left the edge yet would flip again on the next frame and get stuck.
5. **A gentle landing rests.** With gravity, a landing slower than `gravity × REST` stops dead instead of bouncing. Otherwise a ball would make smaller and smaller bounces forever, each one too small to see. `REST` is 0.05 seconds' worth of gravity, so with a gravity of 2000 anything landing slower than 100 px/s rests.

It returns the new body and `edges`, the list of edges it hit this frame, which Bounce uses for the squash and the `onBounce` callback. A box with no room in one direction (`maxX === 0`) holds it still that way, rather than bouncing every frame.

## 3. Friction

Friction takes away a share of the speed over time, the way air and rolling resistance slow a real ball.

The obvious way to write it is `vx *= 0.98` every frame. But that depends on the frame rate: a 120 Hz screen runs it twice as often as a 60 Hz one, so the same Bounce would slow down twice as fast. `step()` instead multiplies by `Math.exp(-friction * seconds)`. Multiplying powers of e adds their exponents, so however a second is split into frames, the total comes to `Math.exp(-friction)`:

- 60 frames: `e^(−f × 1/60)` multiplied 60 times is `e^(−f)`.
- 120 frames: `e^(−f × 1/120)` multiplied 120 times is also `e^(−f)`.

So one second always leaves the same share of the speed:

That's why the prop's description says friction 1 "loses about two thirds of its speed a second". Friction never quite reaches zero on its own: with no gravity, a Bounce with friction drifts slower and slower without stopping.

## 4. Bounciness

At each bounce, the speed into the edge is multiplied by `bounciness`: 1 keeps all of it, 0.5 keeps half, and 0 stops it dead against the edge.

With gravity, how high a ball rises depends on the square of its speed (height = speed² ÷ (2 × gravity)), so each bounce reaches `bounciness²` of the height before. With bounciness 0.8, each bounce is 64% as high as the last. Bounciness and friction both take speed away, but differently: bounciness only at the moment of a bounce, friction all the time.

## 5. The loop: `mover.ts`

`physics.ts` only knows numbers. `mover.ts` connects it to the page. A mover is everything the loop needs to know about one Bounce:

<Code file="experiments/Bounce/mover.ts" name="Mover" />

Every Bounce on the page shares one loop, driven by `requestAnimationFrame`, which the browser calls just before it draws each frame. `move()` adds a mover to the loop, and starts the loop if it isn't running. It returns the function that takes the mover back out, and the loop stops when no movers are left.

<Code file="experiments/Bounce/mover.ts" name="move" />

`tick()` is one frame of the loop:

<Code file="experiments/Bounce/mover.ts" name="tick" />

- **How much time passed.** `now` is the time the browser passes in. The first frame moves nothing (`seconds` is 0), and no frame moves more than `MAX_STEP`, 0.05 seconds. After the tab has been in the background, a frame can come seconds later, and without the cap everything would leap across the screen at once.
- **Read everything, then write everything.** Measuring an element's size (`offsetWidth`) right after moving another one forces the browser to recalculate the layout of the page, and doing that once per Bounce per frame is slow. So `tick()` measures every mover first, then calls `step()` for each, then writes all the new positions.
- **Interactions.** Bounce on its own has none. Another experiment can add one with `interact()`: Magnets does, to push magnets towards and away from each other (`push`), and stop them overlapping (`settle`).
- **Bounce callbacks last.** `onBounce` can re-render React components, so it runs after everything has been placed.

These are the small helpers it uses:

<Code file="experiments/Bounce/mover.ts" name="room" />

`room()` is how far the element can travel: its box's size minus its own size, because `x` and `y` are its top left corner.

<Code file="experiments/Bounce/mover.ts" name="place" />

`place()` moves the element with a CSS `transform` rather than `left` and `top`. A transform doesn't change the page's layout, so the browser can move it without recalculating anything else.

<Code file="experiments/Bounce/mover.ts" name="still" />

`still()` holds a mover still when it's paused, or when the visitor has asked for reduced motion. That's set in CSS, by `--experiment-decoration` (section 7).

## 6. The component: `Bounce.tsx`

The component's job is to create a mover, keep it in step with the props, and get out of the way: React never re-renders to move the dot. The loop writes the transform directly.

<Code file="experiments/Bounce/Bounce.tsx" name="Bounce" />

- **Joining the loop once.** The first `useLayoutEffect` has an empty dependency list (`[]`), so it creates the mover and calls `move()` once, when the component appears, and stops it when the component goes away.
- **Changing props without restarting.** The other effects change that same mover in place: a new `startX` or `startY` moves it, a new `speed` or `angle` gives it a new velocity, and every render copies in the current gravity, bounciness, friction, and `paused`. Changing bounciness mid-flight doesn't reset where it is or how fast it's going.
- **Why `useLayoutEffect`.** It runs before the browser paints, so the mover is in place before anything can see it, or send it a pointer event.
- **Two elements.** The outer `span` is moved by the loop's `transform`. The inner `bounce__content` is the one that squashes. If both used the same element, the squash animation and the loop would overwrite each other's transform.

### The squash

When `step()` reports an edge, Bounce briefly squashes the content flat against that edge and lets it spring back, with the Web Animations API (`element.animate`). `transformOrigin` pins the squash to the edge it hit, so it flattens against the wall instead of shrinking towards its middle.

### Dragging and throwing

With `grabbable`, pressing on it sets `held`, so the loop leaves it alone, and the pointer moves it instead. While it's held, Bounce keeps the positions from the last tenth of a second. On release, the throw velocity is how far it moved in that time, divided by the time:

## 7. Reduced motion and pausing

Anything that moves for more than five seconds needs a way to pause it (WCAG 2.2.2), so Bounce has a `paused` prop.
